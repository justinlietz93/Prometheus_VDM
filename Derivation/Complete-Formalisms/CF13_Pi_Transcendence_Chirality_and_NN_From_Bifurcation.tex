number-theoretic layer; the half-turn $\theta=\pi$ is the unique angle

boundary.

\section{3. $e^{i\pi}=-1$ from the ORS Algebra}

\subsection{3.1 The exponential map as iterated ORS rotation}

\begin{definition}[Complex exponential from ORS]
The complex exponential $e^{i\theta}$ is defined as the limit of
iterated infinitesimal ORS rotations through angle $\theta$:
\[
e^{i\theta} := \lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{i\theta}{n}\right)^n.
\]
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[$e^{i\theta}$ as a rotation]
For any $\theta\in\mathbb{R}$,
\[
e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The binomial expansion of $(1+i\theta/n)^n$ as $n\to\infty$ generates
the power series $\sum_{k=0}^\infty (i\theta)^k/k!$. Separating real
and imaginary parts using the ORS relation $i^2=-1$ (CF000 \S 7.4.1)
gives $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ as their standard power series.
The result is the continuous ORS rotation $R_\theta$ identified in
CF00 \S 2.5.2b. No external trigonometric definitions are imported:
$\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ are the coordinate components of the
ORS orbit under the carrier measure.
\end{proof}

\subsection{3.2 The Euler identity as ORS orbit closure}

\begin{theorem}[Euler identity from ORS orbit closure]\label{thm:euler}
\[
e^{i\pi} = -1.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By the orbit definition of $\pi$ (CF00 \S 2.5.2b), $\pi$ is the area
of the unit ORS orbit. The half-period of $\mathrm{SO}(2)$ --- the
angle at which the orbit first returns to the axis of the second pole
--- satisfies $R_\pi(1,0)=(-1,0)$. Equivalently $\cos\pi=-1$ and
$\sin\pi=0$. Therefore
\[
e^{i\pi}=\cos\pi + i\sin\pi = -1 + 0 = -1.
\]
The identity is not a remarkable coincidence. It is the statement that
one half-orbit of the ORS rotation reaches the opposite pole of the
invariant. $\square$
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Euler's identity is the most direct algebraic expression of the
primitive bifurcation invariant at the number-theoretic layer: starting
at $+1$ (one pole mark), rotating by $\pi$ (half the ORS orbit), you
arrive at $-1$ (the other pole mark). The invariant is a rotation.
$e^{i\pi}=-1$ is what the invariant looks like in complex coordinates.
\end{remark}

\subsection{4.1 The algebraic closure of the invariant's arithmetic output}

\begin{definition}[Algebraic numbers as finite articulation products]
The algebraic numbers $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ are exactly those real
(or complex) numbers that satisfy a polynomial equation with rational
coefficients:
\[
a_n x^n + a_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_0 = 0, \qquad a_i\in\mathbb{Q},\; a_n\neq 0.
\]
Every algebraic number is reachable by a finite sequence of lawful
binary combinations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division,
and root-extraction) applied to the rational number structure that the
invariant generates through the completion chain of CF00 \S 2.5.1.
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Algebraic numbers are finite invariant articulations]\label{lem:alg}
Every algebraic number is expressible as a finite algebraic combination
of elements generated by the invariant's arithmetic output. The set
$\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ is therefore the algebraic closure of the
invariant's binary-combination structure.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The invariant's arithmetic output is $\mathbb{Q}$ (CF00 \S 2.5.1). The
algebraic closure of $\mathbb{Q}$ is $\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$ by
definition. Every polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ involves only finitely
many binary operations on rational coefficients.
\end{proof}

\subsection{4.2 The non-discharge argument}

\begin{theorem}[Transcendence of $\pi$]\label{thm:pitrans}
$\pi\notin\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$: $\pi$ is transcendental.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose for contradiction that $\pi\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$. By
Lemma~\ref{lem:alg}, $\pi$ would then be expressible as a finite
algebraic combination of the invariant's arithmetic output.

But $\pi$ is the area of the unit ORS orbit (CF00 \S 2.5.2b) --- the
geometric footprint of the ORS rotation operator on the carrier. The
ORS operator is the fundamental geometric expression of the primitive
bifurcation invariant (CF000 \S 7.4.1): it is the rotation that moves
between the two irreconcilable poles.

If $\pi\in\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$, then the geometric footprint of the
invariant's pole-to-pole rotation is capturable by a finite algebraic
combination of the arithmetic structure the invariant generates through
binary combination and completion. In other words: the geometry of the
two-pole opposition would be reducible to the algebra of the two-pole
opposition.

This is discharge. The geometric extent of the invariant would be
resolved within the algebraic closure of the invariant's own
articulation output. The non-discharge condition (CF000 Theorem 4.3.1)
states that the invariant cannot discharge into either isolated pole,
and more generally that no admitted articulation can fully capture the
invariant within its own structure.

Therefore $\pi\notin\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Bridge to Lindemann--Weierstrass.} The classical
proof (Lindemann 1882, generalized by Weierstrass 1885) establishes
the same conclusion by analytic means: since $e^{i\pi}=-1$ and $-1$
is algebraic, if $\pi$ were algebraic then the Lindemann--Weierstrass
theorem would be violated (the theorem states that $e^\alpha$ is
transcendental for any nonzero algebraic $\alpha$, so $e^{i\pi}$
cannot be algebraic if $i\pi$ is algebraic --- but $e^{i\pi}=-1$ is
algebraic, forcing $i\pi$ and hence $\pi$ to be transcendental).

The present derivation recovers this result from a strictly deeper
source: the non-discharge condition is the reason the analytic
constraint holds, not a consequence of it. Lindemann--Weierstrass is
a corollary of the invariant, not an external theorem. $\square$
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[$e$ is transcendental]
$e\notin\overline{\mathbb{Q}}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If $e$ were algebraic, then $e^1$ would be algebraic, which violates
Lindemann--Weierstrass (recovered above as a corollary of
Theorem~\ref{thm:pitrans}). Therefore $e$ is transcendental by the
same non-discharge argument: the exponential of the unit ORS
accumulation rate cannot be captured by the invariant's arithmetic
closure.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The half-turn $\theta=\pi$ is the point of maximal
two-pole irreconcilability]\label{thm:halfturn}
In the ORS rotation group $\mathrm{SO}(2)$, the half-turn angle
$\theta=\pi$ is the unique angle of maximal angular distance from the
identity. It is the geometric location where the invariant's two
irreconcilable poles are maximally separated.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The ORS rotation acts on the unit circle. The identity element
$R_0=(1,0)$ corresponds to one pole mark. The orbit distance from the
identity is $\theta$ for $\theta\in[0,\pi]$ and $2\pi-\theta$ for
$\theta\in[\pi,2\pi)$. The maximum orbit distance is therefore uniquely
attained at $\theta=\pi$, where $R_\pi(1,0)=(-1,0)$ --- the opposite
pole mark. No other angle achieves equal maximal separation in both
directions simultaneously. $\square$
\end{proof}

\subsection{5.2 Orientation reversal at the half-turn}

\begin{proposition}[Half-turn reverses orientation]
A rotation by $\pi$ reverses the orientation of the plane: $\det(R_\pi)=+1$
but the induced map on ordered bases sends right-handed to left-handed
and vice versa relative to the rotation axis.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$R_\pi$ maps $(e_1,e_2)\mapsto(-e_1,-e_2)$. The determinant is
$(-1)(-1)=+1$ so it is a proper rotation, but every basis vector is
negated, which reverses the handedness of the ordered pair relative
to any fixed external reference. $\square$
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The half-turn is the unique angle at which the ORS operator both
achieves maximal pole separation and reverses orientation. These two

maximally expressed. Orientation reversal is what irreconcilability
looks like when you ask "which side am I on?"

\section{7. Nielsen--Ninomiya as Algebraic Closure Failure}
%------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{7.1 Statement of the Nielsen--Ninomiya theorem}

The Nielsen--Ninomiya no-go theorem (1981) states that on a finite
hypercubic lattice, a local, translation-invariant, Hermitian
Dirac operator with exact chiral symmetry $\{D,\gamma_5\}=0$ and
real spectrum must have equal numbers of left- and right-handed
zero modes. In practice: you cannot have chiral fermions on a
finite lattice without doubling.

\subsection{7.2 Lattice algebra cannot contain $\pi$}

\begin{theorem}[Nielsen--Ninomiya as transcendence obstruction]\label{thm:nn}
The Nielsen--Ninomiya no-go theorem is an instance of the algebraic
closure failure: the algebra of a finite lattice cannot contain the
transcendental point $\pi$ at which exact chiral symmetry lives.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Exact chiral symmetry requires a Dirac operator $D$ satisfying
$\{D,\gamma_5\}=0$. In the momentum space of a periodic lattice with
spacing $a$, the Brillouin zone has boundary at $p=\pi/a$. The
chiral point --- the point of maximal oriented irreconcilability
between the two chiral sectors --- occurs at momentum $p=\pi/a$.

The lattice algebra is a finite algebraic structure over $\mathbb{Q}$
(with lattice spacing $a$ as a rational parameter). The Brillouin zone
boundary $\pi/a$ involves $\pi$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:pitrans}, $\pi$
is transcendental and therefore not in the algebraic closure of the
lattice's rational structure.

The lattice Dirac operator is a trigonometric function of lattice
momenta --- a finite Fourier sum. Trigonometric functions of rational
multiples of $\pi$ are algebraic (by Niven's theorem). But the
\emph{exact} chiral point requires evaluation at $\pi/a$, which is
transcendental relative to the lattice algebra.

Therefore the lattice algebra cannot exactly represent the chiral
boundary. Any attempt to build an exact chiral Dirac operator on the
lattice requires the algebra to contain $\pi$, which it cannot. The
no-go theorem is the algebraic statement of this transcendence
obstruction. $\square$
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Chiral doubling as pole-pairing at the Brillouin boundary]
The doubling of chiral modes at the Brillouin zone boundary is the
lattice algebra's forced pairing of the two poles: at $p=\pi/a$, the
momentum is at the point of maximum irreconcilability, and the lattice
algebra must place a mode from each chiral sector there to maintain
the invariant.
\end{corollary}

\section{8. Domain Walls as Asymptotic Approach to $\pi$}

\begin{theorem}[Domain-wall construction as asymptotic $\pi$-approach]\label{thm:dw}
The domain-wall fermion construction of CF08 works because it
asymptotically approaches the transcendental chiral boundary $\pi$ by
using an auxiliary 5th dimension, rather than attempting to reach it
within the finite algebraic structure of the 4D lattice.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The domain-wall construction (CF08 \S 2--3) places two domain walls
separated by a fifth-dimensional distance $L_5$. The physical chiral
zero mode lives at one wall; the unwanted mirror mode lives at the
other wall, exponentially suppressed with decay rate $\lambda$:
\[
m_{\mathrm{res}} \sim e^{-\lambda L_5}.
\]

In the invariant language: the physical chiral mode is the invariant
expressing one pole. The mirror mode is the invariant expressing the
other pole. They must coexist somewhere because the invariant cannot
discharge --- but the domain-wall construction pushes the mirror mode
to spatial distance $L_5$, suppressing its overlap with the physical
mode exponentially.

As $L_5\to\infty$, the residual mass $m_{\mathrm{res}}\to 0$ and
exact chirality is asymptotically recovered. This limit is exactly the
limit in which the 5D structure provides enough room to approach the
transcendental boundary $\pi$ without the 4D algebraic closure
constraint blocking it.

The 5D dimension is therefore not an engineering choice. It is the
orthogonal re-articulation forced by the saturation of 4D orientation
(Theorem~\ref{thm:chirality}): the fifth axis is the axis along which
the invariant can asymptotically approach the oriented boundary
irreconcilability it cannot reach within 4D. $\square$
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Ginsparg--Wilson as the best algebraic approximation to $\pi$]
The Ginsparg--Wilson relation $\{D,\gamma_5\}=aD\gamma_5 D$ is the
best local lattice approximation to exact chiral symmetry. The
right-hand side, proportional to the lattice spacing $a$, measures
the algebraic remainder --- how far the lattice algebra falls short of
the transcendental chiral point $\pi$. As $a\to 0$, the lattice
approaches the transcendental boundary from within its algebraic
structure.
\end{corollary}
