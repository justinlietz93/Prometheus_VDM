Here’s an interesting update: researchers have produced a strikingly precise measurement of the cosmic microwave background (CMB) temperature at an intermediate redshift — and the result strongly supports the standard cosmological model.

### What’s new

A team from Keio University in collaboration with National Astronomical Observatory of Japan (NAOJ) used archival data from the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA) to analyse molecular‑absorption lines toward the quasar PKS1830‑211, whose light passes through a foreground galaxy at redshift **z = 0.89**. They derived a CMB temperature at that epoch of **5.13 ± 0.06 K**. ([Keio University][1])
That value closely matches the expectation from the standard relation
[
T_{\rm CMB}(z) = T_{\rm 0} \times (1+z)
]
(where (T_{\rm 0} ≈ 2.725) K today and (z = 0.89) gives ≈ 5.14 K). ([arXiv][2])
Because the uncertainty is just ±0.06 K (≈1.2%), it’s the **most precise** intermediate‑redshift determination of this kind to date. ([Keio University][1])

### Why it matters

* It tests a fundamental prediction of Big Bang cosmology: that as the universe expands, the CMB cools proportionally to (1 + z). The fact that it holds (within tight error bars) adds confidence to our understanding of cosmic thermal history.
* Previous measurements at similar redshifts existed (for example an earlier value at z = 0.89 of 5.08 ± 0.10 K) but with larger uncertainties. ([arXiv][3])
  This new result refines the precision considerably (~40% improvement) compared to the prior best for that redshift. ([arXiv][4])
* It places strong constraints on alternative cosmological models that would predict deviations from the standard scaling. The team fitted a model with (T_{\rm CMB}(z) = T_0 (1+z)^{1-β}) and found β consistent with zero within very small error bounds. ([arXiv][4])

### How they did it

The method uses molecular absorption lines (specifically HCN J=2‑1, 3‑2, 4‑3, 5‑4) in the absorber galaxy:

* They assume the gas is dominated by the CMB radiation field in its excitation (i.e., collisions or local radiation fields are negligible) so level populations reflect the background CMB temperature. ([arXiv][2])
* They carefully accounted for systematic effects: uncertainties in how much of the background continuum the absorber covers, time‑variability of the quasar, optical‐depth saturation and biases from non‑uniform clouds. ([arXiv][4])
* The final number of 5.13 ± 0.06 K comes from weighting the excitation temperature over a high‑quality velocity component of the absorption lines. ([arXiv][2])

### What’s next / Limitations

* Though excellent, the measurement still relies on assumptions (e.g., LTE, neglect of collisional excitation) that become more challenging at higher redshifts and different environments. The authors note that further improvements and expansion to higher z are desirable. ([arXiv][4])
* Extending this kind of precision to redshifts (z>1) or (z>2) would open stronger tests of cosmology, especially for late‑time or exotic models that depart from the simple scaling. The paper points to upcoming instruments (upgraded ALMA, SKA etc) for future work. ([arXiv][2])
* Because the absorber geometry (lens galaxy, covering factor) is complex, there remains some systematic uncertainty — though the authors have reduced them significantly.
